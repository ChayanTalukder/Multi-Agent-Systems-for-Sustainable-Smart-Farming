# RQ3 — Agricultural, Resource, Livestock, and Environmental Outcomes

**RQ3:** *What agricultural, livestock-welfare, resource-efficiency, and environmental outcomes are reported for MAS-based smart-farming approaches?*

The strongest outcome evidence concerns **resource management and efficiency**. Resource-related outcomes are reported in **29 of 34 publications**, representing 23 of 27 study families. Common benefits include reduced irrigation demand, improved water-use efficiency, lower grid-energy purchases, greater renewable-energy utilisation, improved labour/task allocation, and more efficient nutrient or manure management.

Agricultural and operational outcomes are reported in 32 publications, including crop yield and soil-moisture control, farm income, climate-adaptation decisions, harvest performance, and resource-allocation effectiveness. However, not all studies measure direct crop-production improvements; many evaluate operational or decision-support outcomes instead.

Livestock-specific evidence is comparatively limited, appearing substantively in only **four independent study families**. Reported outcomes include herd viability, grazing behaviour, animal-condition dynamics, and behavioural welfare-risk indicators.

Environmental outcomes are reported in **21 publications across 17 study families**. Water scarcity and irrigation demand are the dominant environmental themes, while fewer studies evaluate soil carbon, nutrient losses, land degradation, manure pollution, greenhouse-gas emissions, or other ecosystem impacts. Sustainability is addressed directly in 21 publications, indirectly in 11, and is not substantively addressed in two.

Trade-offs are reported in **26 publications**, commonly involving yield versus water use, profit versus environmental objectives, efficiency versus fairness, or performance versus computational cost.

Overall, MAS show particular value for balancing agricultural performance with constrained-resource use. However, because most studies are simulation-based, the reported benefits primarily represent model- or experiment-supported potential rather than extensively validated real-world farm impacts.
