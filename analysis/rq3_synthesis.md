# RQ3 — Agricultural, Resource, Livestock, and Environmental Outcomes

**RQ3:** *What agricultural, livestock-welfare, resource-efficiency, and environmental outcomes are reported for MAS-based smart-farming approaches?*

The primary analysis uses **53 publications**, with comparison against **44 study families**.

A populated outcome field indicates that an outcome was described. It does **not** automatically indicate that the outcome improved.

## Outcome evidence coverage

| Outcome indicator | Original 34 | Final 53 | 44 families |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Agricultural outcomes described | 32 (94.1%) | 51 (96.2%) | 42 (95.5%) |
| Livestock outcomes described | 5 (14.7%) | 7 (13.2%) | 7 (15.9%) |
| Resource-efficiency outcomes described | 29 (85.3%) | 48 (90.6%) | 40 (90.9%) |
| Environmental outcomes described | 21 (61.8%) | 38 (71.7%) | 32 (72.7%) |
| Environmental mitigation mechanism described | 22 (64.7%) | 41 (77.4%) | 34 (77.3%) |
| Quantitative results reported | 28 (82.4%) | 47 (88.7%) | 39 (88.6%) |
| Trade-offs described | 26 (76.5%) | 45 (84.9%) | 38 (86.4%) |

Agricultural and resource-efficiency outcomes therefore dominate the evidence base.

Livestock-specific biological or welfare outcomes are much less common, even when livestock or dairy farms are part of the application setting.

| Outcome-direction classification | Publications |

These are reviewer interpretations of the extracted evidence, not causal effect estimates.

## Main outcome patterns

Water and irrigation studies commonly report water use, irrigation efficiency, allocation equity, crop production, farmer utility, or drought response.

Energy applications report electricity cost, grid demand, renewable utilisation, and peer-to-peer trading outcomes.

Task-allocation and robotics studies report execution time, staff time, workload, travel distance, or scheduling performance.

Socio-ecological studies often report economic, livelihood, soil, water-quality, policy, land-use, or resource-distribution outcomes.

## Environmental mitigation mechanisms

Reported mechanisms include:

- precision and predictive irrigation;
- scarcity-aware water allocation;
- groundwater and catchment governance;
- manure and nutrient management;
- pesticide/IPM policy;
- soil-conservation measures;
- renewable-energy scheduling and P2P exchange;
- crop-livestock resource exchange;
- land-use and WEF-nexus planning.

These mechanisms should be described as modelled or evaluated interventions unless the publication provides evidence of actual operational impact.

## Important adjudicated examples

**S03:** an approximately 11% gross-margin loss applies to a specific simulated seed-policy scenario. It is not evidence that MAS generally reduce farm profitability.

**S25:** actual farm teams achieved shorter execution time in the reported comparison. Any staff-time advantage must be reported together with that trade-off.

**S33:** harvest-allocation performance is algorithm- and metric-specific and should not be transferred to S25.

**S48:** different water-allocation rules favour different combinations of profitability and fairness; there is no universally superior strategy.

**S49 and S53:** these publications belong to the same study family, SF49. Reported simulated improvements such as higher yield proxy, lower water use, and improved water-use efficiency therefore constitute one related evidence line rather than independent replication.

**S04:** response quality, routing accuracy, or hallucination metrics for the decision-support system do not demonstrate improved cattle welfare in operational farming.

## Trade-offs

Trade-offs occur frequently across the literature.

Examples include:

- profitability versus equitable resource allocation;
- crop output versus water conservation;
- staff effort versus task execution time;
- environmental restrictions versus farm income;
- renewable-energy value versus food production;
- optimisation performance versus computational complexity;
- individual incentives versus collective sustainability.

## Direct answer to RQ3

Agricultural MAS report substantial evidence concerning resource allocation, irrigation, farm productivity, operational efficiency, and environmental modelling.

However, the evidence is heterogeneous and frequently simulation-based.

Many studies report promising results, but a large part of the corpus contains mixed, scenario-dependent, or trade-off-sensitive outcomes.

The review therefore supports the conclusion that MAS can provide useful mechanisms for analysing and coordinating sustainable agricultural decisions, but it does **not** establish universal real-world sustainability improvements across the corpus.
